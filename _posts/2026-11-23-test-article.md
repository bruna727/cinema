All About Eve is kind of a mess.

I believe the film is trying to tell a story about female empowerment while incorporating villains, manipulation, and elaborate schemes. Unfortunately, I don't think it executes that combination particularly well.

The film starts by portraying Margo as unnecessarily hostile toward Eve, initially because she suspects Eve is trying to steal her boyfriend, and later simply because she finds her irritating and is jealous of her youth. However, the story eventually validates Margo's paranoia and rude behavior by revealing that Eve really does have ulterior motives. The problem is that Margo had no legitimate reason to know this beforehand. She merely happened to be correct, and her prior hostility, rooted in patriarchally conditioned jealousy, is retrospectively vindicated by sheer coincidence.

What initially seemed like a grounded examination of female beauty standards, aging, and the rivalry between women encouraged by the patriarchy suddenly becomes a story filled with schemes, betrayals, and dramatic plot twists. I have to admit this was somewhat disappointing, as it felt like the film had abruptly turned into a Mexican telenovela. The intrigues are entertaining enough, though, and my biggest problems lie elsewhere: primarily in the resolution of Margo's character arc and, to a lesser but still noticeable extent, the film's treatment of female ambition.

The film clearly makes an effort to present feminist ideas, with its female characters openly criticizing the expectations imposed on them by the patriarchy. Margo's speech in the car and Karen's reflections on realizing, even as a young girl, that her experience was different from that of boys are particularly clear examples. Yet, despite these sentiments, the film seems to associate female ambition almost exclusively with negative implications or consequences.

Margo, she finds happiness through marriage, seemingly abandoning the insecurities surrounding her age and career that had plagued her throughout the entire story. Eve achieves professional success, but she's the villain, and her accomplishments remain subject to Addison's control. Phoebe, explicitly a high school student, appears at the end as a visual suggestion that the cycle of female rivalry and manipulation will continue indefinitely. And Karen, the only other prominent female character, is a housewife.

Do you see the pattern?

None of these choices would necessarily be problematic in isolation. There's nothing inherently wrong with a woman finding happiness in marriage, being a housewife, or pursuing professional success through morally questionable means. But when all these portrayals are presented together, they paint a troubling picture. The film, presumably unintentionally, either demonizes female professional ambition or fails to offer a convincing example of a woman finding genuine fulfillment outside traditional romantic expectations.

The most baffling example by far is Margo's conclusion. She already had loyal, supportive friends, particularly Karen, and was already in a loving relationship with Bill. She wasn't lacking companionship, affection, or emotional support. Of course, having these things doesn't automatically make someone emotionally secure, but the film never convincingly explains what changes for her to suddenly overcome her insecurities.

One could argue that Margo finally learns to appreciate what she already has and stops defining herself through youth and professional success. But why does this realization seemingly happen only when she accepts Bill's marriage proposal? Marriage is largely a symbolic formalization of a relationship that was already loving and committed. It doesn't make Bill love her more or eliminate the pressures of aging as an actress. Yet the film treats it almost like a magical problem solver, making her anxieties disappear without adequately developing the emotional transformation that would justify it.

In fact, this reminds me of the pressure experienced by women in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries who feared being labeled "old maids." Marriage was often treated as a marker of social legitimacy and personal success, regardless of whether a woman actually desired married life beyond its social status. And Margo's ending seems to validate that very fear. Despite already having love and companionship, she only achieves genuine happiness once she fulfills the societal expectation of becoming a wife. There's something particularly ironic about a film that criticizes the pressures imposed on women seemingly presenting conformity to those very expectations as the solution to its protagonist's problems.

There is, however, one genuinely positive aspect of the film's portrayal of women: the friendship and sisterhood between Margo and Karen.

Karen consistently supports Margo emotionally and even defends her against Lloyd when he wants to give Eve a role despite his long-standing friendship with Margo. She also confronts Margo when necessary, but I've always found those moments appropriate to the circumstances. She seems genuinely motivated by a desire to help. Although we don't see Margo supporting Karen quite as much, since Margo is the one struggling throughout most of the story, their friendship still feels reciprocal.

However, there's one unfortunate detail: Karen is Margo's only close female friend, and she isn't a professional colleague. On its own, this would be completely unremarkable, but alongside the film's other portrayals of female ambition and rivalry, it contributes to the same troubling picture. The only genuinely supportive female friendship exists outside direct professional competition, while the relationship between women pursuing success in the same field is defined by jealousy and betrayal.

Despite that, I still find their friendship incredibly wholesome and enjoyable to watch. Their affection and loyalty feel genuine, and their relationship remains one of the film's greatest strengths.

Ultimately, it's this portrayal of female friendship, combined with the fact that the film's intrigues remain entertaining despite my problems with their execution and implications, that makes me give All About Eve 3 stars rather than a lower rating.
