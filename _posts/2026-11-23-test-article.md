All About Eve is kind of a mess.

I believe the film is trying to tell a story about female empowerment while incorporating villains, manipulation, and elaborate schemes. Unfortunately, I don't think it executes that combination particularly well.

Some people might interpret *All About Eve* as an exploration of the psychological consequences of working in performance, particularly the toll it can take on one's mental health and sense of identity. However, I would argue that a lot of the struggles Margo describes over dedicating herself so completely to her profession that she feels disconnected from her true self, are applicable to virtually anyone who becomes overly consumed by their career. There's nothing inherently exclusive to actors or artists about losing yourself in your work.

Where Margo's profession as an actress actually becomes relevant is in the film's exploration of beauty standards. As an actress, she faces particularly intense beauty standards and the pressure of aging in an industry that places so much value on women's appearances. This is where the film begins to intertwine its commentary on career ambition with its treatment of womanhood.

I actually think Margo's struggle with having become so absorbed in her career that she's forgotten how to live a life beyond it is handled well, and Bette Davis portrays her emotional breakdowns marvelously.

My problems with the film begin here:

First of all, the film starts by portraying Margo as unnecessarily hostile toward Eve, initially because she suspects Eve is trying to steal her boyfriend, and later simply because she finds her irritating and is jealous of her youth. However, the story eventually validates Margo's paranoia and rude behavior by revealing that Eve really does have ulterior motives. The problem is that Margo had no legitimate reason to know this beforehand. She merely happened to be correct, and her prior hostility, rooted in patriarchally conditioned jealousy, is retrospectively vindicated by sheer coincidence.

What initially seemed like a grounded examination of female beauty standards, aging, and the rivalry between women encouraged by the patriarchy suddenly becomes a story filled with schemes, betrayals, and dramatic plot twists. I have to admit this was somewhat disappointing, as it felt like the film had abruptly turned into a Mexican telenovela. The intrigues are entertaining enough, though, and my biggest problems lie elsewhere: primarily in the resolution of Margo's character arc and, to a lesser but still noticeable extent, the film's treatment of female ambition.

But where my biggest problem lies is in what I consider perhaps the most baffling aspect of *All About Eve*: Margo's conclusion, which seems to undermine much of what the film attempts to say about women and the expectations imposed on them by society.

Throughout the story, Margo struggles with insecurities surrounding her age, career, and identity. Yet, by the end, she seemingly overcomes these anxieties through her decision to marry Bill. The problem is that Margo already had loyal, supportive friends, particularly Karen, and was already in a loving relationship with Bill. She wasn't lacking companionship, affection, or emotional support. Of course, having these things doesn't automatically make someone emotionally secure, but the film never convincingly explains what changes for her to suddenly overcome her insecurities.

One could argue that Margo finally learns to appreciate what she already has and stops defining herself through youth and professional success. But why does this realization seemingly happen only when she accepts Bill's marriage proposal? Marriage is a symbolic formalization of a relationship that was already loving and committed. It doesn't make Bill love her more, provide her with companionship she previously lacked, or eliminate the pressures of aging as an actress. Yet the film treats it almost like a magical solution to her problems, making her anxieties disappear without adequately developing the emotional transformation that would justify it.

What makes this particularly frustrating is how closely Margo's conclusion resembles the very social pressures that feminism has historically criticized. Throughout the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, women were often taught to fear becoming "old maids," as though remaining unmarried were a personal failure, regardless of their accomplishments, relationships, or actual happiness. Marriage wasn't simply about finding love or companionship; it was treated as a milestone women were expected to achieve to be considered truly fulfilled.

And isn't that precisely what Margo's ending seems to suggest?

Margo already has romance. She already has friendship. She already has professional success. What she doesn't have is the formal status of being a wife. Yet somehow, that distinction appears to be what finally allows her to find peace with herself. The implication is particularly troubling because it makes Margo resemble the very woman who fears becoming an "old maid" not because she lacks meaningful relationships, but because society has convinced her that those relationships are somehow insufficient without the institution of marriage.

In other words, Margo's problem isn't presented as a lack of love, but her conclusion makes it seem as though love only becomes enough once it takes a socially approved form. Rather than questioning why marriage should carry such enormous weight in a woman's sense of fulfillment, the film appears to accept that expectation without examining it.

And this is where I find the contradiction especially glaring. *All About Eve* gives its female characters moments in which they explicitly recognize and criticize the limitations society imposes on women. Yet its central female character ultimately finds happiness by conforming to one of those very expectations, and the film presents this as a satisfying resolution rather than something worth questioning.

I'm not suggesting that Margo shouldn't have married Bill. The issue is that the film seems to treat becoming a wife as the answer to insecurities that marriage itself has no clear reason to resolve. After spending so much of its runtime exploring Margo's emotional turmoil, it offers a conclusion that seems to validate the very societal conditioning its feminist commentary should be challenging. 

What makes Margo's conclusion even more frustrating is that it draws attention to other aspects of the film that I otherwise wouldn't have thought twice about.

*All About Eve* clearly makes an effort to present feminist ideas, with its female characters openly criticizing the expectations imposed on them by the patriarchy. Margo's speech in the car and Karen's reflections on realizing, even as a young girl, that her experience was different from that of boys are particularly clear examples. Yet Margo's ending made me reconsider how the film portrays its other female characters.

Eve achieves professional success, but she's the villain, and her accomplishments remain subject to Addison's control. Phoebe, explicitly a high school student, appears at the end as a visual suggestion that the cycle of female rivalry and manipulation will continue indefinitely. Meanwhile, Karen, the only other prominent female character and Margo's only female friend in the story, is a housewife.

Do you see the pattern?

Under different circumstances, I wouldn't have found any of these choices particularly noteworthy, let alone problematic. There's nothing inherently wrong with portraying a woman as a housewife, depicting an ambitious woman as morally corrupt, or exploring rivalry between women. In fact, I probably wouldn't have questioned any of these portrayals if Margo's story had reached a more convincing conclusion.

But once the film presents marriage as the apparent solution to Margo's insecurities, these otherwise unremarkable details begin to take on a different meaning. Together, they create a troubling pattern in which female professional ambition is repeatedly associated with dissatisfaction, manipulation, or moral corruption, while fulfillment seems tied to traditional expectations of womanhood.

It's this broader pattern, which I might never have noticed without Margo's ending, that makes the film's feminist message feel so contradictory to me.

Ultimately, I'm rating *All About Eve* a 6.5/10 because, while its intrigues are engaging, they're nothing particularly remarkable. At times, the film feels more like a soap opera you'd find your mother watching in the living room than anything especially sophisticated. Still, while the film's exploration of the consequences of career obsession is merely decent, it's elevated by Bette Davis's wonderful performance and charisma. And despite my reservations about the implications of Margo's best friend being a housewife, their friendship is genuinely wholesome and a pleasure to watch.
